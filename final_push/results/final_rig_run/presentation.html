Why this matters

PID runs the process industry, and most loops are not well tuned

PID loops are tuned once, at commissioning, and then left alone. Real processes drift: fouling, wear, ambient changes, supply changes. The tuning goes stale.

97%
of regulatory controllers use PID, in a survey of more than 11,000 controllers in refining, chemicals and pulp & paper
Desborough & Miller (Honeywell), 2002
> 95%
of all industrial control loops are PID (most of them PI). Their tuning decides plant performance
Åström & Hägglund, Advanced PID Control, ISA, 2006
30% · 30% · 20%
of loops ran in manual · increased variability because of poor tuning · used factory default settings
Ender, “Process control performance: not as good as you think”, Control Engineering, 1993

Why re-tuning is expensive

  • Every re-tune needs a step test: the process is deliberately disturbed and runs off-spec while you wait for it to settle.
  • Slow thermal processes take hours per test. A skilled engineer has to plan and supervise it.
  • So it gets postponed. The plant runs with stale gains, so you get more variability, more energy use and more actuator wear.

Our own rig shows the same thing

Characterising our heater at 4 supply voltages took 522 minutes (8.7 hours) of step testing (87 + 88 + 246 + 101 min). The rig's time constant is about 15 minutes, so each test needs 1–1.5 h to settle.

And the result is voltage-dependent: MATLAB's own pidtune gives a 5× different Kp at 2 V than at 5 V. A fixed tuning is only right at one operating point.

Survey figures are as reported in the cited publications.

Illustrative cost of manual re-tuning example, not measured

A site with 500 PID loops where 20 % drift enough each year to need a re-tune, at 4 engineer-hours per loop including the step test, spends 400 engineer-hours a year on re-tuning, plus the off-spec product during each test. Thermal loops like ours take hours per test. An agent that keeps the loops tuned removes that recurring cost and the variability of the months between re-tunes.

The numbers above are assumptions chosen to show the scale. Replace them with your plant's numbers.

Physical setup

The rig we tune: Nano R3 + 24 V cartridge heater + NTC sensor

We control the temperature at the farthest point from the heater: the hardest one, with the largest dead time (40–65 s). A0 = NTC temperature, A1 = current, A2 = supply voltage, so the agent sees heater power directly.

Gate: D6 → 100 Ω → STP55NF06 gate, 10 kΩ gate-to-GND pull-down, common ground between the heater supply and the Nano. The 25V sensor sits across the supply (before the MOSFET). Calibration constants are at the top of the .ino.

What the data told us

  • R ≈ 3.7 Ω → power ∝ V²: from 2 V to 5 V the loop gain grows 6.8×.
  • Heat loss grows with temperature: K drops from 7.9 to 6.8 °C/W.
  • One nonlinear model fits all four runs to 0.40 °C RMS:
    τ·dT/dt = K₀·P(t−θ) − ΔT(1+βΔT)
    K₀ = 8.51 °C/W · β = 0.0054 /°C · τ = 1141 s · θ = 40 s
  • The log parser re-joins samples CoolTerm split over two lines and removes thermocouple glitches.

Step 3 · The agent

TD3: a slow “brain” that tunes the fast “reflex”

Design
Fast loopPID_v1 on the Nano, every 1 s
Slow loopTD3 policy every 10 s → ΔKp, ΔKi, ΔKd
Observation (10)error, temperature slope, mean PWM, PWM chatter, Kp/Ki/Kd, measured supply V, headroom, ΔT
Actionlog-space steps of a multiplier on the SIMC gains computed from the model at the measured voltage and setpoint: Kp, Ki × 0.25–4, Kd × 0.05–2 (hard clamp again on the Nano)
Reward−|e| − 2·overshoot − λ·|Δu| (track tightly, don't overshoot, don't hammer the heater)
Training2 seeds × 300 k decisions, γ = 0.99. Each episode draws a new plant (K₀, β, τ, θ, R, ambient, sensor noise and resolution) and a supply/setpoint change; 40 % start near SIMC, 60 % from gains up to 4× wrong
Model selectionfixed test suite (8 scenarios × 3 starting tunings), score = IAE ÷ IAESIMC; best: 0.88
Why TD3twin critics (no Q over-estimation), delayed actor, target smoothing: safer for a heater

Test-suite score every 20 k steps (lower = better, 1.00 = SIMC accuracy). It swings by up to 0.4 between checkpoints, so the kept model is chosen on the fixed suite.

What we learned on the rig

v1 agent failed on the rig; v2 fixes the causes

The first agent (v1) drove its gains to the limits: Kp → 300, Ki → 0.002. That is an on/off controller with no integral action. On the rig it left a permanent ±0.85 °C oscillation at 40 °C.

Gains could roam 2.5 decades (Kp 1–300, Ki 0.002–3); the agent found a corner with Ki ≈ 0Anchored gains: SIMC(V, SP) × 0.25–4. Integral action can never be switched off; zero action = SIMC

What the agent learned

Supply jumps 3 V → 5 V (heater power × 2.8)

  • At the red line the supply jumps. Loop gain rises 2.8×.
  • The agent lowers its gains: the correct direction (more voltage → less gain). On the real rig it cut Kp from ~130 to ~25 and Ki from 0.6 to 0.13 after the same kind of jump.
  • Lowest peak deviation of all controllers: 1.42 °C vs Cohen–Coon 1.64, SIMC 2.35, MATLAB 3.86.
  • IAE: TD3 431 vs Cohen–Coon 375, ZN 520, SIMC 1124, MATLAB 2783, with 5× less heater switching than Cohen–Coon.

How the model plugs into the Python script

From one serial line to new PID gains, every 10 seconds

1 · Nano prints
T:41.25,SP:50,E:8.75,U:100,V:4.01,I:1.07,P:4.3,G:20,0.05,100
→
2 · parse()
T, SP, PWM, V (A2), I (A1), current gains
→
3 · build_obs()
same 10 values as in training, over a 10 s window
→
4 · TD3 policy
models/td3_best/best_model.zip → action ∈ [−1,1]³
→
5 · new gains
SIMC(V, SP) × multiplier, clipped → K:Kp,Ki,Kd → Nano SetTunings()
# run_on_rig/live_tuner.py (core loop, simplified)
model = TD3.load("models/td3_best/best_model.zip")
while True:
    d = parse(ser.readline())            # 1 line per second
    window.append(d)
    if len(window) >= RL_EVERY:          # every 10 s
        obs, z, base = build_obs(window, T_prev, V, cfg)  # base = SIMC(V, SP)
        act = model.predict(obs, deterministic=True)[0]
        z = clip(z + STEP_Z * act, -1, 1)   # log-space multipliers
        kp, ki, kd = gains_from_z(z, base)   # SIMC × 0.25–4, clamped
        ser.write(f"K:{kp},{ki},{kd}\n")  # Nano applies live
    print(T, SP, PWM, V, I, P, Kp, Ki, Kd)   # live table

Why it transfers to the real rig

  • Same PID code in the simulator and on the Nano (PID_v1 behaviour: bumpless gain change, anti-windup).
  • Same observation code: build_obs() mirrors ThermalPIDEnv._obs().
  • Domain randomisation over gain, nonlinearity, τ, θ, resistance, ambient and noise covers the reality gap.
  • Double safety: bounds in Python and on the Nano; heater off above 110 °C. If the laptop disconnects, the last gains stay active. If the heater stops obeying the PID (current at PWM 0 %), the agent is frozen.
Run it on the rig

Exactly which files to run

1 · Arduino Nano (once)

  • Install the CH340 driver (Windows) so the Nano gets a COM port
  • Library Manager: PID by Brett Beauregard (the only library needed)
  • Open run_on_rig/arduino_nano_rl_pid/arduino_nano_rl_pid.ino
  • Board Arduino Nano, Processor ATmega328P (Old Bootloader) → Upload
  • Serial Monitor at 9600: T: = room temperature, V: = supply voltage, I: = multimeter. Then close it (and CoolTerm).

2 · Laptop

git clone https://github.com/dkchauhan2006/rl-pid-autotuner
cd rl-pid-autotuner
python -m venv .venv && .venv\Scripts\activate
pip install torch --index-url https://download.pytorch.org/whl/cpu
pip install -r requirements.txt

3 · Run (auto finds the CH340 Nano)

# a) watch only: prints the gains it WOULD send
python run_on_rig/live_tuner.py auto --setpoint 35 --volts 4.0 --shadow
# b) live RL tuning (+ live plot, SIMC twin alongside)
python run_on_rig/live_tuner.py auto --setpoint 35 --volts 4.0 --compare simc --plot
# c) comparison runs with fixed gains
python run_on_rig/live_tuner.py auto --setpoint 35 --fixed simc --volts 4.0
# d) statistics of a run vs all classical rules
python tools/analyze_final_run.py --log run_on_rig/logs/<run>.csv

Prints every second: t · T · SP · err · PWM% · V · I · P | Kp · Ki · Kd · action. Logs to run_on_rig/logs/*.csv.

To test the claim: settle at the setpoint, switch the supply (we used 3.3 → 5.2 → 4.1 V), then repeat with --fixed simc and compare the logs. --volts is only needed if the A2 voltage sensor is not connected.

Honest limitations

  • One real-rig run so far (35 °C, two supply disturbances). Classical rules were compared on a twin calibrated to that run, not re-run on the rig.
  • Low supply (3.3 V) was the weak spot on the rig (±0.5 °C limit cycle), traced to a wrong ambient value in the live tuner; fixed, still to be confirmed on the rig.
  • At a single fixed operating point Cohen–Coon / ZN are hard to beat on this easy, lag-dominant loop (θ/τ ≈ 0.04). Our edge is adapting when conditions change and gentler actuation.
